## Q2 2026 insights

**1. Portfolio growth and workload need separate stock, rate and flow measures.** On Slide 13, management says: “Production volumes more than offset prepayment activity, leading to continued portfolio growth.” The same slide reports 2,753 thousand loans serviced, a 4.1% owned-portfolio 60+ delinquency rate versus 4.2% in Q1, an 11.6% owned-portfolio actual CPR versus 13.7%, completed modifications of $2,850 million versus $1,400 million, and EBO volume of $1,218 million versus $632 million. The slide footnote says the delinquency rate is based on loan count, not UPB, and defines CPR as conditional prepayment rate.

The 10-Q’s period-end servicing table keeps the populations distinct: owned MSRs/MSLs of $488.097617 billion, loans held for sale of $7.656960 billion, subservicing of $235.314288 billion, and total loans serviced of $731.068865 billion (HTML table 235, rows 7–19, printed page 70). Completed modifications and EBO volume are period flows; UPB and loans serviced are stocks. The filing separately says servicing expense rose $14.2 million year over year in Q2, primarily from servicing-advance loss provisions tied to higher delinquent balances, a larger portfolio and a larger proportion of 90+ delinquencies (HTML text line 3101, printed page 71). Slide 22 says MSR servicing advances were approximately $526 million at June 30, down from $545 million at March 31 because a smaller percentage of delinquencies were later stage. Those comparisons are different periods and should not be collapsed into one credit conclusion.

**2. Custodial-balance income helped while prepayment realization and MSR funding costs moved the other way.** Slide 14 says: “Average custodial deposit balances increased 7% from the prior quarter, driving a $14 million increase in revenue.” It also says: “Realization of MSR cash flows was down from the prior quarter due primarily to lower prepayment speeds” and “Interest expense was up $15 million from 1Q26 due to higher average financing balances for MSRs.” The slide’s driver table shows Q2 earnings on custodial balances, deposits and other income of $119 million versus $105 million in Q1; MSR cash-flow realization of $(323) million versus $(355) million; and interest expense of $(140) million versus $(125) million.

The 10-Q gives the year-over-year explanation separately: net interest expense increased $10.6 million in Q2 and $33.9 million in the first half, primarily from financing a larger MSR investment and interest shortfalls on repayments of loans serviced for Agency securitizations, partly offset by higher loan-held-for-sale interest income and placement fees (HTML text line 3074, printed page 70). Loan servicing fees from non-affiliates increased primarily because the servicing portfolio grew (HTML text line 3044, printed page 68). The slide’s quarter-over-quarter explanation and the filing’s year-over-year explanation should remain labeled as such.

**3. MSR valuation remains highly exposed to rates, prepays and hedging costs.** Slide 15 says that in Q2 “gains from changes in fair value inputs on MSR were more than offset by hedging declines and costs,” that hedge costs were meaningfully higher because of elevated interest-rate volatility, and that yield-curve shape, volatility and mortgage-basis changes can affect the realized hedge ratio. Its attributed-performance view shows MSR changes of $110 million, hedge and related losses of $(187) million, and net $(77) million before realization of cash flows.

The 10-Q provides a different, more granular bridge: valuation-input changes of $118.307 million, hedging results of $(185.581) million, realization of cash flows of $(322.814) million, and total MSR/MSL fair-value change net of hedging of $(390.088) million (HTML table 234, rows 7–11). The filing explains that fair-value changes have cash-flow-realization and market-input components, with derivatives and principal-only stripped MBS used to moderate market-input effects (HTML text lines 3048–3057, printed page 68). It also says an OAS model was adopted beginning in Q3 2025 to model multiple interest-rate paths instead of a single assumed path (HTML text line 2570, printed page 31).

As of June 30, 2026, the filing’s point-in-time MSR sensitivity table estimates a $(118.629) million fair-value change for a +5% prepayment-speed shift, $(75.196) million for a +5% OAS shift, and $(56.323) million for a +5% annual per-loan servicing-cost shift (HTML table 265, rows 5–7, printed page 80). The filing expressly says these modeled sensitivities should not be viewed as earnings forecasts. The presentation bridge and filing bridge have different scopes; they must not be added together.

**4. Liquidity depends on collateral and short-term financing execution.** Slide 16 reports: “As of June 30, 2026 total liquidity including cash and amounts available to draw with collateral pledged was $4.0 billion.” It also says unsecured senior notes provide liquidity at low fixed rates, with the first maturity in February 2029. The $4.0 billion is a total-company figure that includes cash and collateral-backed draw capacity; it is not cash-only or servicing-only liquidity.

The 10-Q says a significant amount of borrowings has 30-to-364-day maturities and facilities are renewed before maturity (HTML text line 3153, printed page 74). It describes MSR and servicing-advance facilities using VFNs, term notes and term loans secured by Fannie Mae and Ginnie Mae MSRs and advances (line 3155). It also reports a May 27, 2026 issuance of $300 million of Term Notes at a 2.25% spread due May 2031, alongside a partial redemption of $300 million of Term Notes at a 3.20% spread due March 2029 (line 3156). Margin deficits can require cash or additional assets on the notice date or within one business day (line 3161). This makes collateral availability, facility renewals and margin-call timing useful oversight questions even though the filing does not say a margin call occurred in Q2.

## FY2025 annual context

**5. Advance reimbursement and recoverability are structural servicing risks.** The FY2025 10-K’s risk factors state: “If a loan serviced by us is in default or becomes delinquent, the repayment to us of the advance may be delayed until the loan is repaid or refinanced or a liquidation occurs. Federal, state or local regulatory actions may also result in an increase in the amount of servicing advances that we are required to make, lengthen the time it takes for us to be reimbursed for such advances and increase the costs incurred while the loan is delinquent.” The adjacent paragraph adds that FHA, VA and USDA loans “may not be fully insured or guaranteed under each of the VA, the FHA and the USDA government loan programs.” These are [the annual filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1745916/000110465926018142/pfsi-20251231x10k.htm), document aaaf62ff1b5145d2d2198f86, content hash `bc2ebcde63126b5bd400a4c415dafcaf00adb1ded456f309dc2cb558af291921`, HTML text lines 3088–3089, Risk Factors—Risks Related to Mortgage Production and Servicing / “We are required to make servicing advances” (printed page 21).

The accounting policy explains the mechanism: “The Company is contractually responsible for making the payments required to protect its beneficial interest holders’ interests in the properties collateralizing their loans and may, therefore, be required to advance amounts in excess of insurer or guarantor reimbursement limits. Therefore, the Company provides a valuation allowance on the servicing advances for these amounts in excess of amounts that are expected to ultimately be recovered from the loans’ insurers, guarantors or beneficial interest holders.” In the Consolidated Balance Sheets, servicing advances, net were $589.542 million at December 31, 2025, including a $103.574 million valuation allowance; $406.825 million was identified as pledged to creditors (reported in thousands). These excerpts are from Note 3—Significant Accounting Policies / Servicing Advances, HTML text line 4411, printed page F-13, and table 247 row 13, printed page F-4. The filing says the allowance uses historical collection and loss experience, current conditions and reasonable forecasts, and that advances are written off when deemed unrecoverable (HTML text line 4413, printed page F-13). This annual disclosure identifies advance needs, reimbursement timing and ultimate recoverability as separate monitoring items; it does not establish a Q2 2026 reimbursement shortfall or regulatory curtailment.
